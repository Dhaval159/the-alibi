THE ALIBI

Complete Game Design, Product Requirements & Development Specification

Project Codename: THE ALIBI
Platform: Android
Game Type: Online Multiplayer Social Deduction / Mystery Party Game
Player Count: 4-15 players
Target Match Duration: 7-10 minutes
Primary Language: English (designed for future localization)
Development Approach: AI-assisted, documentation-first, modular development

1. PROJECT VISION

1.1 What is THE ALIBI?

THE ALIBI is an online multiplayer mystery-solving party game designed for groups of friends.

Players join a private room, receive fictional characters, secret information, personal objectives and individual alibis. A fictional crime has taken place, and everyone must work together to discover the culprit.

However, not everyone is innocent.

One player is secretly assigned as the culprit. Other players have different motives, secrets, relationships and pieces of information.

Players must investigate evidence, interrogate one another, identify contradictions, defend themselves and ultimately accuse the person responsible.

The game is inspired by the social deduction experience of Among Us and Mafia, combined with detective investigations, murder mystery party games and psychological bluffing.

The central design philosophy:

Every player should have something interesting to contribute.

Everyone should have a reason to hide information.

The culprit should have a realistic opportunity to deceive others.

Innocent players should have enough evidence to uncover the truth.

Winning should depend on deduction, observation and communication rather than random guessing.

THE ALIBI should feel like a murder mystery party that fits inside everyone's Android phone.

1.2 The problem we are solving

Groups of friends frequently meet physically but struggle to find interesting multiplayer games beyond Among Us, Mafia or simple party games.

THE ALIBI aims to provide a fresh experience with:

Short, replayable multiplayer mysteries.

Interesting characters and fictional storylines.

Secret roles and personal information.

Real conversations between friends.

Evidence-based deduction.

Different mysteries with different mechanics.

A simple room-code-based multiplayer experience.

1.3 Target audience

Primary audience:

Groups of friends aged 16 and above.

College students.

Friend groups who regularly meet.

People who enjoy Mafia, Among Us, detective stories and mystery-solving games.

The game should be accessible to casual players while offering enough depth for people who enjoy psychological deduction.

3. CORE GAMEPLAY

3.1 Basic gameplay concept

Every match contains:

One fictional crime.

One victim.

One fixed underlying solution.

One human-controlled culprit.

Multiple suspects.

Individual character profiles.

Private information.

Evidence and investigative clues.

A final accusation.

The mystery has a predetermined truth.

The game must never randomly change the culprit, motive or underlying timeline during an active match.

Players discover the truth through investigation.

3.2 Match flow

Phase 1: Create or join a room

A player creates a private room.

The game generates a six-digit room code.

Other players enter the code and join.

The lobby displays:

Player names.

Player avatars.

Connection status.

Ready status.

Host indicator.

Selected mystery.

Number of participants.

The host can start once the minimum player count is reached.

Phase 2: Mystery selection

The host selects an available mystery.

Every mystery must specify:

Minimum supported player count.

Maximum supported player count.

Difficulty.

Expected duration.

Available character configurations.

Only mysteries supporting the current player count can be selected.

Phase 3: Character assignment

Each player receives a private character card.

A character card contains:

Character name.

Age (fictional).

Occupation.

Relationship with the victim.

Personality description.

Background.

Location during the incident.

Personal alibi.

Private information.

Secrets.

Knowledge of other characters.

Special objectives, if applicable.

Publicly available information.

Characters should feel like actual people rather than simple game roles.

Phase 4: Incident briefing

All players receive the public incident briefing.

It includes:

Victim identity.

Location.

Approximate time.

General circumstances.

Initial investigation findings.

The culprit is not revealed.

Phase 5: Investigation

Players receive access to the evidence system.

Evidence is unlocked in stages.

Evidence types include:

Physical evidence.

Documents.

Photographs.

Witness statements.

Medical reports.

Security records.

Timeline information.

Digital records.

Contradictory statements.

Players can inspect evidence and discuss their findings.

Phase 6: Interrogation

Players can:

Discuss freely in person.

Send private in-app messages.

Ask structured questions.

Challenge another player's statement.

Reveal selected information.

Withhold information.

Defend their alibi.

The game should support both free discussion and structured interrogation.

Phase 7: Final accusation

Every player privately selects the person they believe is responsible.

Players can optionally select a reason or supporting evidence.

Votes are locked after submission.

The game then reveals the results.

Phase 8: Case resolution

The complete truth is revealed.

The resolution includes:

Actual culprit.

Motive.

Timeline.

What actually happened.

Explanation of important evidence.

Contradictions.

Why other suspects appeared suspicious.

The game displays individual and group results.

4. PLAYER ROLES AND CHARACTER SYSTEM

THE ALIBI is character-driven.

Players should not receive only a generic role such as Innocent or Detective.

They should receive an actual fictional identity.

4.1 Character categories

Culprit

Exactly one human player is assigned as the culprit.

The culprit:

Knows they committed the crime.

Knows their actual motive.

Receives their private instructions.

Must defend their alibi.

Can lie about their involvement.

Can selectively reveal information.

Must avoid being correctly identified.

The culprit must be a human participant in every match.

There are no NPC culprits.

Innocent characters

These players did not commit the crime.

However, they can have:

Financial motives.

Personal conflicts.

Hidden relationships.

False statements.

Embarrassing secrets.

Reasons to conceal their activities.

Being innocent does not mean being completely honest.

Witness characters

These players possess important information.

They may have:

Seen something.

Heard a conversation.

Observed suspicious behaviour.

Accessed a particular location.

Discovered a relevant document.

Witnesses should not automatically know the culprit.

Special characters

Some mysteries can include additional roles:

Detective.

Doctor.

Security officer.

Journalist.

Lawyer.

Investigator.

These are character-specific responsibilities, not necessarily separate team factions.

4.2 Character information structure

Every character must have two information layers.

Public information

Name.

Occupation.

Relationship with the victim.

Public background.

Basic timeline.

Private information

Personal secrets.

Actual whereabouts.

Private conversations.

Knowledge of other characters.

Hidden motives.

Important evidence.

Special objectives.

A player should never automatically see another player's complete character profile.

4.3 Personal objectives

Some characters should receive secondary objectives.

Examples:

Protect a family secret.

Prevent a particular document from being revealed.

Avoid being associated with the victim's financial problems.

Get another player to trust you.

Ensure a particular piece of evidence is discovered.

Personal objectives add complexity without changing the actual crime solution.

The culprit's main objective is to avoid being correctly identified.

5. MYSTERY DESIGN SYSTEM

5.1 Pre-authored mystery architecture

All launch mysteries must be written and reviewed before being included in the game.

Do not depend on live AI generation during matches.

Reasons:

Consistent mystery quality.

Predictable difficulty.

No generation delays.

No runtime AI API costs.

Reliable evidence and timelines.

Easier multiplayer synchronization.

No accidental changes to the actual solution.

Mysteries must be stored as structured JSON content.

The Android client should download or retrieve the relevant case data from the backend.

5.2 Mystery components

Every mystery must contain:

Case metadata.

Incident description.

Victim profile.

Full character roster.

Actual culprit.

Actual motive.

Underlying timeline.

Character relationships.

Individual character profiles.

Secret information.

Evidence records.

Witness statements.

Contradictions.

Interrogation questions.

Player configurations.

Final solution.

Explanation of the deduction path.

5.3 Mystery difficulty

Use three initial difficulty levels.

Easy

Clear evidence.

Fewer interconnected secrets.

More direct contradictions.

Suitable for new players.

Medium

Multiple suspicious characters.

Several interconnected clues.

Misleading information.

Requires comparing statements.

Hard

Multiple layers of deception.

Complicated timelines.

Hidden relationships.

Evidence that must be interpreted together.

Innocent characters with convincing motives.

No obvious culprit.

5.4 Mystery replayability

The game should support replayability through:

Different valid player configurations.

Different character combinations.

Different evidence discovery sequences.

Optional secondary objectives.

Multiple presentation orders.

However, randomization must never alter the underlying truth.

Do not create random evidence that makes the solution inconsistent.

Every mystery contains a full fictional cast.

However, the number of actual players can vary.

For example, a case may contain 10 characters but only 6 human players.

The remaining four characters become NPCs represented through:

Police statements.

Witness interviews.

Written reports.

Medical records.

Security footage descriptions.

Personal documents.

Recorded conversations.

These NPCs must still have consistent knowledge, motives and timelines.

Characters should be categorized as:

Essential
Must appear in every supported configuration.

Flexible
Can be human-controlled or converted into an NPC.

Supporting
Can be omitted or converted into background evidence.

Certain critical witnesses may also be essential, depending on the mystery.

6.5 Validated player configurations

Every case must contain predefined valid configurations for supported player counts.

The system must verify:

Culprit is included.

All necessary evidence is available.

The mystery remains logically solvable.

No missing character creates an impossible deduction.

NPC statements are consistent.

No configuration accidentally reveals the culprit immediately.

The system must not simply randomly remove characters.

6.6 Fifteen-player support

The platform supports up to 15 human players.

Individual mysteries can have different cast sizes.

A case that has only 10 characters must not automatically accept 15 participants.

For a case to support 15 players, its authored cast must contain enough meaningful player characters.

The lobby must filter cases according to their actual supported player count.

8. INTERROGATION SYSTEM

The interrogation system combines free discussion with structured interaction.

8.1 Free discussion

Players can talk in person or through their own external voice communication.

The game does not require everyone to type.

8.2 Structured interrogation

The app provides questions that players can direct at other characters.

Examples:

Where were you at the time of the incident?

When did you last see the victim?

Why did you hide this information?

Can anyone confirm your alibi?

Why does your statement contradict this document?

Structured questions can have:

Character-specific questions.

General questions.

Evidence-related questions.

Follow-up questions.

The questions should not reveal the answer directly.

8.3 Private messaging

Players can privately send text messages to one another.

This is useful for:

Secret alliances.

Sharing information.

Bluffing.

Negotiating.

Misleading other players.

The server must enforce valid message delivery and room membership.

8.4 Statement challenges

A player can challenge another player's statement by selecting relevant evidence or a contradiction.

The app should record the challenge.

It should not automatically declare the challenged player guilty.

Players must interpret the information.

9. WINNING CONDITIONS

9.1 Innocent players

The innocent team wins when the group correctly identifies the culprit through the final accusation.

9.2 Culprit

The culprit wins when the final accusation does not correctly identify them.

The culprit should also receive a personal result explaining whether their secondary objectives were completed.

9.3 Voting

Voting must be:

Private.

Simultaneous.

Locked after submission.

Validated by the server.

Every eligible player must vote.

For launch, use a simple majority system.

If there is no majority, the result should be a failed accusation and the culprit wins.

A tie must never randomly select someone as the culprit.

9.4 Results

The result screen should show:

Actual culprit.

Player accusations.

Correct and incorrect votes.

Individual objectives.

Important deductions.

Case explanation.

Match statistics.

Potential statistics:

Best Detective.

Best Bluff.

Most Suspicious.

Most Misleading.

Best Evidence Finder.

Most Accused.

These awards should be based on actual recorded game events, not arbitrary guesses.
